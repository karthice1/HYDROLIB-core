\title{How far can one gauge mark and one radar image narrow parameter uncertainty in an open-data flood model? Sensitivity analysis of the 2018 Periyar flood}

\author[kahe]{Karthik V\corref{cor1}}
\ead{karthik.vasanthakumar@kahedu.edu.in}
\author[kahe]{Roopa D}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{abstract}
Flood models of data-scarce tropical basins rest on global data whose errors are rarely quantified together. We built an open-data two-dimensional D-Flow FM model of the lower Periyar River, Kerala, for the August 2018 flood with the HYDROLIB-core library. Validating the first model against the 12.4~m high-flood level (HFL) at Neeleswaram and a Sentinel-1 flood map exposed three structural errors and one missing tributary that Morris screening had missed. On the final model, 180 Morris runs rank channel depth, floodplain roughness and rating-curve error first for peak flooded land; sea level is negligible. A 600-run Latin hypercube over the five leading factors was conditioned on the HFL, with a $\pm$1.0~m tolerance set from an assumed datum and observation-error scenario, and on the radar map used as a lower bound. The 73 accepted runs give a peak flooded land of 146--243~\kmsq: a conditional ensemble-range contraction of 62\% against a prior interval of equal expected coverage, or 77\% at $\pm$0.5~m. The HFL does most of this; the radar test alone contracts the range by 20\%, only by rejecting dry runs. A 200-run ensemble varying all eight factors gives 73\%, so fixing three factors did not inflate the result. Reruns show that the release formulation, travel times and gauge operator matter less than the tolerance, but that the gauge level responds unevenly to small forcing changes. Spatial agreement with the radar is poor (critical success index at most 0.10). A drainage diagnosis attributes the excess post-peak flooding to closed depressions of the surface-model terrain and slowly draining low land; it remains an unresolved structural discrepancy. The contraction is conditional on the model structure, factor ranges and acceptance rule, is not a reduction of predictive error, and the model is not validated for operational use. What transfers is the workflow, not the numbers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The floods and landslides of August 2018 in Kerala took 433 lives \citep{pdna2018}. At Neeleswaram the Periyar peaked at about 8,800~\cumecs{} on 16 August and reached EL~12.4~m, 1.3~m above the previous record of 1974 \citep{cwc2018}. Events of this kind are increasingly modelled in two dimensions with open global data: the Copernicus DEM \citep{esa2021}, ESA WorldCover \citep{zanaga2022}, the JRC Global Surface Water record \citep{pekel2016} and global canopy-height maps \citep{tolan2024} make a 2D model possible almost anywhere. Each of these data sets carries errors, however. A digital surface model (DSM) is not a terrain model, and a coarse water mask misses channel geometry.

Global sensitivity analysis (GSA) is the standard way to find which of these uncertain inputs matter. Morris elementary effects \citep{morris1991}, summarised by the mean absolute effect $\mu^*$ \citep{campolongo2007}, screen many factors at low cost, within the broader framework of \citet{saltelli2008}. Conditioning on observations, as in GLUE \citep{beven1992}, then keeps only the parameter sets consistent with what was seen. Studies of 2D flood models usually assume a validated model and treat the observations separately. In data-scarce basins the model structure itself is uncertain, and few studies show how GSA and sparse observations interact, or report how validation changed the model before the GSA was run.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible open-data workflow in Python (HYDROLIB-core) from global rasters to an ensemble of D-Flow FM runs, with every model version, run and score archived.
\item Evidence that validation against two cheap observations exposed structural errors that GSA alone did not, and a check that the factor ranking survives the last structural change.
\item A test of how far a single HFL mark and a single post-peak radar scene, under dense tree cover, can narrow the predicted flood area, with the narrowing quantified against ensemble size, acceptance rule, likelihood weighting, factor ranges, fixed factors, release formulation and observation operator.
\item Structural tests (terrain, channel mask, grid, tide) and a full contingency analysis against the radar map that bound what the parameter ensemble can claim.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related work}

\paragraph{Uncertainty and sensitivity in flood inundation models}
\citet{teng2017} review the main classes of inundation model and how their uncertainty is analysed. \citet{pianosi2016} give a systematic review of sensitivity analysis for environmental models with a practical workflow; we follow its separation of factor screening (here Morris) from the conditioning of predictions. \citet{hall2005} applied variance-based (Sobol') sensitivity analysis to a raster inundation model of a River Thames reach checked against a radar flood outline, and found that channel roughness dominated the predicted outline and depth; splitting the channel into subreaches showed where the variance came from. \citet{pappenberger2005} carried uncertainty from ensemble rainfall forecasts through a rainfall-runoff model into LISFLOOD-FP, weighting ten roughness sets by their likelihood. \citet{savage2016} showed that grids coarser than about 50~m change flow paths and shift the best-fitting parameters, and that probabilistic flood maps lose reliability beyond about 100~m. This bears directly on our 200~m grid.

\paragraph{Conditioning on flood extent}
GLUE \citep{beven1992} rejects parameter sets that fail an observation and keeps the rest as behavioural. \citet{aronica2002} applied it to an inundation model using binary wet/dry pattern information, and \citet{bates2004} updated flood inundation likelihoods with flood extent data in a Bayesian scheme. \citet{montanari2009} used water stages derived from radar flood extents to calibrate and sequentially update a coupled hydrologic-hydraulic model. These studies use extents over largely open floodplains. In the lower Periyar most of the floodplain is under tree cover, so the radar extent can only bound the model from below, and we test what that weaker information can still constrain.

\paragraph{Terrain data}
Global elevation models such as the Copernicus DEM are surface models that include trees and buildings. \citet{hawker2022} produced FABDEM, a 30~m version of the Copernicus DEM with forests and buildings removed. We instead remove a fraction of canopy height and treat that fraction as an uncertain factor, so its effect on the flood can be measured rather than fixed in preprocessing, and we test a bare-earth proxy as a structural alternative.

\paragraph{The 2018 Kerala flood}
\citet{mishra2018} placed the August 2018 event in its hydroclimatic context. \citet{sudheer2019} modelled the Periyar basin with HEC-HMS and found that the Idukki peak release (about 1,860~\cumecs) was smaller than the flow of the nearly uncontrolled Perinjankutty tributary (about 3,500~\cumecs). Reservoir operation lowered the Neeleswaram peak by about 17\% compared with a no-dam case, and earlier emptying of the reservoirs would have lowered it by a further 16--21\%, still well above the bankfull capacity of about 4,200~\cumecs. They estimated a return period of about 142 years for the Neeleswaram flood. These studies are hydrological. We found no two-dimensional inundation model of the lower Periyar for 2018 whose uncertainty has been analysed.

\paragraph{What is new here}
Screening followed by behavioural selection is established practice, and we use standard methods (Morris, Latin hypercube, GLUE-style acceptance). Earlier studies differ from ours in four ways. \citet{hall2005} and \citet{pappenberger2005} quantified parameter sensitivity or uncertainty of a model taken as structurally adequate, and \citet{aronica2002}, \citet{bates2004} and \citet{wood2016} conditioned models on radar extents over largely open floodplains, where the extent is a strong two-sided constraint; \citet{wood2016} showed that channel depth and friction trade off against each other when calibrated on such extents. \citet{stephens2012} and \citet{schumann2009} examined how uncertainty in the satellite data limits such tests. To our knowledge, these studies do not report how much of a range contraction each observation provides, and they do not use the observations first to revise the model structure. Our contribution lies in how the standard methods are ordered, reported and tested. First, the sparse observations are used to diagnose and revise the model structure before the GSA, and the screening is repeated on the final structure to show that the ranking is not an artefact of the revision. Second, the contraction of the predicted range is defined on a like-for-like basis, reported with every interval behind it and split into the contribution of each observation, so that a reader can see that the gauge mark, not the radar scene, does the work. Third, a radar scene taken five days after the peak over a mostly forested floodplain is used only as a one-sided lower bound, and we show quantitatively that the test favours wetter runs. Fourth, structural variants and a drainage diagnosis inside the model show where the parameter ensemble stops being informative, and why. We are not aware of a study that combines these for an open-data model of a forested floodplain.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Hydrodynamic model}
D-Flow FM \citep{kernkamp2011} (computational kernel 1.2.184, built from the Deltares source code) runs on a 200~m rectilinear grid of 32,337 cells in UTM zone 43N, clipped to land below 20~m plus a 400~m corridor around the channel. The run spans 13 August 00:00 to 21 August 07:00 IST. The Copernicus DEM tiles are reprojected and aggregated to 200~m by averaging (and separately by minimum), the canopy height by averaging, the JRC water occurrence by maximum, so that any permanent water marks a cell as channel and narrow reaches stay connected, and WorldCover by mode. Every cell with water occurrence above 50\% is channel, with its bed set a channel depth below the lowest DSM value in the cell. Water cells not connected to the river are kept as channel cells; they exchange water with the floodplain only where they touch it. The floodplain bed is the mean DSM minus a canopy factor times the mean canopy height. Manning $n$ follows WorldCover classes (0.025--0.10). The upstream boundary takes the Neeleswaram discharge, which already includes the dam releases and the intervening catchment. A second inflow on the north edge carries the Chalakudy River, using the CWC Arangaly record (14--17 August), which then recedes in proportion to the Periyar. No local rainfall or other lateral inflow is included. Numerical settings: implicitness $\theta$ = 0.55, maximum Courant number 0.7, maximum time step 30~s (mean 28.7~s in the nominal run), wetting/drying threshold $10^{-4}$~m, bed level at faces from the mean of node values. In the nominal run $6.92\times10^9$~m\textsuperscript{3} enters through the boundaries, of which $4.27\times10^9$~m\textsuperscript{3} is river inflow (3.54 at Neeleswaram and 0.73 on the Chalakudy) and the rest is tidal inflow at the sea edges; $6.24\times10^9$~m\textsuperscript{3} leaves, storage rises by $0.68\times10^9$~m\textsuperscript{3}, and the cumulative volume error is $1.7\times10^{-4}$~m\textsuperscript{3}. This shows that the solver conserves mass; it is not evidence that the hydraulics are right, which only the observations can test. All model files are written with HYDROLIB-core.

\subsection{Uncertain factors}
Table~\ref{tab:factors} lists the eight factors and their ranges. All are sampled from independent uniform distributions. Three ranges rest on the size of known errors: the rating-curve error ($\pm$15\% on the whole Neeleswaram hydrograph, a common allowance for high-flow ratings), the two release multipliers ($\pm$20\%, for reporting and digitising error) and the sea-level offset (surge and datum uncertainty). The roughness multipliers span the usual spread of tabulated Manning values around the land-cover defaults. Channel depth and the canopy factor have no direct observation in this basin, so their ranges are chosen to explore model behaviour over all plausible values, and we read the conditioned values of these two factors with that in mind. The rating error and the release multipliers are not physically independent, because the Neeleswaram record already contains the releases. The upstream boundary flow is therefore built so that no water is counted twice:
\begin{equation}
Q_\mathrm{bnd}(t) = q_\mathrm{r}\,Q_\mathrm{N}(t) + (m_\mathrm{I}-1)\,R_\mathrm{I}(t-8\,\mathrm{h}) + (m_\mathrm{A}-1)\,R_\mathrm{A}(t-4\,\mathrm{h}),
\label{eq:forcing}
\end{equation}
where $Q_\mathrm{N}$ is the digitised Neeleswaram hydrograph, $q_\mathrm{r}$ the rating multiplier, $R_\mathrm{I}$ and $R_\mathrm{A}$ the reported Idukki and Idamalayar releases, lagged by their travel times to Neeleswaram, and $m_\mathrm{I}$, $m_\mathrm{A}$ the release multipliers. At $m = 1$ the releases add nothing beyond what the gauge already records; $m \neq 1$ represents an error of $\pm$20\% in the released share of the gauged flow, so the factor changes the released water, not the total gauged flow plus a second copy of the releases. The Chalakudy inflow is not perturbed. Lagged releases make up 32\% of the Neeleswaram flood volume (675 and 460~\Mcm{} of 3,541~\Mcm) and 25\% of the peak (8,803~\cumecs). Over the factor ranges the upstream volume spans 0.79--1.21 times nominal. The rating multiplier stands for a systematic error of the stage--discharge relation at high flow, which scales the whole gauged hydrograph; the release multipliers stand for an error in how much of that gauged flow came from the dams, which can be wrong without the gauge being wrong. Their physical independence is therefore partial, and the multiplicative form below tests the other limiting case. The travel times of 8~h from Idukki and 4~h from Idamalayar are not published and are assumed. Because a lag acts only on the correction $(m-1)R$, it has no effect at $m = 1$; halving the lags or increasing them by half changes the inflow by at most 92~\cumecs{} (1.0\% of the peak) at the ends of the release ranges and does not move the time of the peak. We also reran the 23 runs accepted at $\pm$0.5~m with lags of 4 and 2~h and of 12 and 6~h (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}). Because Eq.~\ref{eq:forcing} lets the rating error act on the whole gauged flow but not on the release correction, we also ran the 23 runs accepted at $\pm$0.5~m with a multiplicative form, $q_\mathrm{r}[Q_\mathrm{N} + \sum (m-1)R]$; the two forms differ by at most 83~\cumecs{} (0.9\% of the peak) at any time and factor setting (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}).

\subsection{Screening}
A Morris design with 20 trajectories, 4 levels and the SALib default jump $\Delta = 2/3$ (180 runs, seed 42) was generated with SALib 1.6.0 \citep{herman2017}, and 95\% confidence intervals on $\mu^*$ come from 100 bootstrap resamples. $\mu^*$ measures the overall influence of a factor; a large $\sigma$ shows that the effect varies across the factor space, through non-linearity or interaction, without separating the two or giving the share of interaction. Each run is reduced to peak flooded land, peak flood volume, mean maximum depth, time to peak, the Neeleswaram peak level and the validation scores below. Cells that are wet at the start are excluded, so the metrics describe land that the flood inundated.

\subsection{Validation and conditioning}
\label{sec:validation}
\paragraph{HFL} The modelled level is the highest hourly median water level over the 10 channel cells within 500~m of the centre of the inflow section (76.49\textdegree E, 10.172--10.198\textdegree N), the reach where the Neeleswaram gauge lies, and it is compared with the HFL as a peak level. The exact gauge position is not published and the 200~m grid does not place a cell on it, so we take the channel cells within 500~m and their median at each hour, so that no single poorly placed cell drives the comparison. The highest hourly median is compared with the HFL because the CWC value is the maximum level recorded at the gauge during the event (16 August, 15:00 IST, with the peak discharge); the model's median peak in the nominal run comes 2~h later. In the nominal run the peak level varies by 0.26~m among these cells (14.62--14.88~m). Because this observation operator is a modelling choice, every run of the supplementary ensembles also records the peak level under six alternatives: the single nearest channel cell, the median over channel cells within 300, 750 and 1,000~m, and the highest and the lowest single-cell peak within 500~m (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}). A run that matches the HFL under any of these operators matches one level at one place; it does not show that the model's water levels are right elsewhere.

\paragraph{HFL error scenario} We searched three CWC documents for the datum of the Neeleswaram HFL: the 2018 flood study report \citep{cwc2018}, which gives ``EL 12.4~m'' and the earlier HFL of EL~11.105~m (1974); the December 2018 report on the Kerala flood, which fixes warning and danger levels of 9 and 10~m at the site from its cross-section \citep{cwc2018b}; and the 2025 flood-forecasting procedure, which lists the site with warning, danger and highest flood levels of 9.00, 10.00 and 12.40~m \citep{cwc2025}. None names the vertical datum. The CWC reports give reservoir and river elevations as ``EL'' or ``above MSL'', and we assume that the HFL is referred to mean sea level through Survey of India levelling. This is an assumption, not an established fact, and the error model below is therefore a scenario for the size of the mismatch, not a verified observation-error model. The model's vertical datum is that of the Copernicus DEM, the EGM2008 geoid. We represent the mismatch by
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\mathrm{obs} = \sqrt{\sigma_\mathrm{datum}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{cell}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{mark}^2} = \sqrt{0.46^2 + 0.13^2 + 0.10^2} \approx 0.50~\mathrm{m},
\label{eq:hflerror}
\end{equation}
with three components. $\sigma_\mathrm{datum}$ = 0.46~m is the RMS difference between EGM2008 geoid heights and GPS-levelling on Indian levelling benchmarks in south India \citep{srinivas2012}; it describes the EGM2008--levelling mismatch in the region, and it applies to the HFL only if the HFL is tied to that levelling network, which we assume. $\sigma_\mathrm{cell}$ = 0.13~m is half the spread of peak level among the gauge cells, a model-side representation error. $\sigma_\mathrm{mark}$ = 0.10~m is an assumed allowance for recording the peak: gauge levels are typically read at fixed intervals during floods, and near the peak the level changed slowly. The three come from different sources (geodesy, model discretisation, field reading), so we treat them as independent; if the datum error were instead correlated with the DEM's own vertical error, the combined error could be larger. A tolerance of $\pm$1.0~m is about $2\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$ under this scenario and is our central case; $\pm$0.5~m (about $1\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$) is a strict case. Structural error in the modelled level, of which the 0.9~m change between the 100~m and 200~m grids (Section~\ref{sec:structural}) is one measure, is not part of $\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$. Adding it in quadrature gives about 1.0~m, or a $\pm$2$\sigma$ tolerance near 2~m, so we also report $\pm$1.5~m as a structural-error case.

\paragraph{Radar} The Sentinel-1 flood map uses calibrated VV backscatter with a 5\,$\times$\,5 filter, below an Otsu threshold on the post-event scene ($-13.8$~dB) together with a drop of more than 3~dB from 28 July, aggregated from 20~m to the 200~m grid as a flooded fraction. Permanent water (the model channel mask, from JRC occurrence) is removed, and cells with less than half valid radar coverage are excluded. No urban, radar-shadow, layover or vegetation mask was applied to the classification. A cell counts as flooded above a fraction of 0.5: 550 cells (22~\kmsq) pass this, and a further 3,231 cells have a smaller non-zero fraction, so the 22~\kmsq{} is a conservative count of clearly flooded cells rather than an estimate of flooded area. C-band radar largely misses water under tree cover and among buildings, which cover 75.8\% and 14.9\% of the land cells; the radar map is therefore treated as a lower bound on the model's flooded land, not as a target, and the hit-rate test below is restricted to open land (about 9\% of land cells). We had no independent flood map of the same date, so the accuracy of the classification is not known; the threshold tests in Section~\ref{sec:radar} show only how sensitive our results are to it.

\paragraph{Acceptance rule} A run is \emph{accepted} (behavioural in the GLUE sense, which here means consistent with the two acceptance tests, not spatially validated) when its HFL error is within the tolerance ($\pm$1.0~m central, $\pm$0.5~m strict), its flooded land at the radar pass is at least 22~\kmsq, and it floods at least 75\% of the open-land cells (WorldCover cropland, grassland, bare and herbaceous wetland) that the radar shows as flooded. Land means cells that are dry at the start of the run and have valid radar coverage. The 0.75 hit rate is a lenient test of the observed open-land extent, not a skill threshold; because a wetter run floods more of every area, it favours wetter runs, and we report its effect separately (Section~\ref{sec:conditioning}). The radar test is kept despite the low spatial agreement because it is the only constraint on the dry side: a run that matches the HFL but leaves most of the open land that the radar shows as flooded dry is inconsistent with the one spatial observation we have, whatever its CSI. It cannot reject runs that flood too much. CSI is not used for acceptance, because every run overpredicts extent at the pass for the same structural reason (Section~\ref{sec:drainage}) and CSI would then select the driest runs regardless of the HFL. An earlier rule that also capped the model area at three times the radar's rejected every run of the final model (Section~\ref{sec:conditioning}).

\paragraph{Ensemble and what ``constrain'' means} The five leading factors were sampled by two Latin hypercubes, 200 runs (seed 42) and 400 runs (seed 43), with the other three at nominal values. We report the \emph{conditional ensemble-range contraction}
\begin{equation}
C = 1 - \frac{w_\mathrm{acc}}{w_\mathrm{prior}(n)},
\label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
where $w_\mathrm{acc}$ is the full range of peak flooded land over the $n$ accepted runs and $w_\mathrm{prior}(n)$ is the width of the central interval of the prior ensemble with coverage $(n-1)/(n+1)$, the expected coverage of the range of $n$ samples (like-for-like). That coverage is exact in expectation only for $n$ independent draws from a continuous distribution; our accepted runs are a subset of a Latin hypercube selected by the observations, so the matching is approximate. $C$ is conditional on the model structure, the fixed factors, the factor ranges and the acceptance rule, and it is not a calibrated predictive interval or a reduction of predictive error. The raw prior range, the prior 5--95\% interval, the like-for-like interval, the accepted range and the accepted 5--95\% interval are all reported (Table~\ref{tab:intervals}). $C$ complements the accepted 5--95\% interval rather than replacing it: the interval says where the accepted runs lie, while $C$ compares widths on a like-for-like basis, so that the contraction is not inflated simply because a small accepted set has a narrow range. We test $C$ in four ways: a bootstrap in which the 600 runs are resampled with replacement at the run level 5,000 times, the rule is re-applied to each resample and $C$ recomputed with its own $n$ (this measures only the sampling variability of a finite ensemble, not structural, forcing or observation uncertainty); a grid of HFL tolerances (0.25--1.5~m) and hit rates (0.5--0.9); an informal likelihood weighting in place of the yes/no rule, $L = \exp[-\tfrac12(e/s)^2]\,r^p$ for HFL error $e$ and open-land hit rate $r$, with zero weight below 22~\kmsq; $s$ = 0.5~m matches $\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$ and 0.25 and 1.0~m bracket it, and $p$ = 4 is a moderate penalty on low hit rates whose choice we test with $p$ = 0, 1 and 8; and removal of the lowest or highest fifth of each factor's range, as if the range had been set narrower.

\subsection{Structural tests}
Eight structural variants were rebuilt and run at nominal parameters: the nominal model; a bare-earth proxy that takes the lowest 30~m DSM pixel in each 200~m cell (FABDEM could not be downloaded from our computing environment); full canopy removal (factor 1.0); a wider channel mask (water occurrence above 25\%); 100~m and 400~m grids; and the tide scaled by 1.5 or shifted by half an M2 period (6.21~h). Each run is scored against the radar with the full contingency table: hits, misses, false alarms and correct negatives, hit rate, false-alarm ratio, critical success index (CSI), frequency bias and equitable threat score (ETS). The radar map was also rebuilt with the Otsu threshold moved by $\pm$1.5~dB and with change thresholds of 2 and 4~dB, and scored with a cell-fraction cut-off of 0.3 as well as 0.5.

\subsection{Drainage diagnosis}
\label{sec:drainagemethod}
To find why land stays flooded after the peak, we kept the full map output of the nominal run and of the median accepted run and classified every land cell flooded at the radar pass. The spill level of each cell, the lowest water level at which it could drain to the sea over the model terrain, comes from a priority-flood fill of the 200~m bed-level raster from the west and south (sea) edges. A flooded cell is in a \emph{closed depression} when its water level is at or below its spill level and the depression is deeper than 0.1~m, so the water can leave only by infiltration or evaporation, neither of which is modelled; \emph{draining} when it is connected and its level fell by more than 0.1~m in the 24~h before the pass; and \emph{connected, slow} otherwise. Cells next to a channel cell are counted separately and the classes are also reported without them, because there the face bed level that D-Flow FM averages from its nodes mixes channel and floodplain and can sit below the floodplain raster. We test the depression-depth threshold at 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5~m and the level-fall threshold at 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2~m. The classes describe how the model terrain and the simulated levels hold the water; they are derived from the same terrain and simulation, and cannot show the real cause of any one false-alarm cell or that a diagnosed depression is a real isolated water body. The same fill gives the closed-depression storage of the terrain for canopy factors of 0.3--1.0 and the bare-earth proxy. Finally, we ran the nominal model once more with every closed floodplain depression filled to its spill level. This is a diagnostic experiment, not a physically validated representation of the floodplain, whose depressions drain through canals and sluices that the open data do not contain; it tests whether the ponding in the model explains the model's excess flooding.

\subsection{Supplementary ensembles}
\label{sec:suppmethod}
Four further ensembles test choices that the main ensemble held fixed. (i) The 23 runs accepted at $\pm$0.5~m were rerun with the multiplicative release formulation (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), and twice more with shorter (4 and 2~h) and longer (12 and 6~h) release travel times. (ii) Each of those 23 runs was rerun with four Latin hypercube draws (seed 45) of the three factors held at nominal values in the main ensemble (channel roughness, Idamalayar release and sea-level offset), 92 runs. (iii) A 200-run Latin hypercube (seed 44) varied all eight factors. (iv) A 140-run Morris design (20 trajectories) screened the six factors other than the two release multipliers, to test whether the ranking depends on the release formulation. (v) The three runs whose HFL error changed most under the multiplicative form were rerun in both forms (6 runs) to check that the model is deterministic and to record every observation operator.

\paragraph{What each observation contributes} The HFL does most of the work: alone it gives $C$ = 0.49 at $\pm$1.0~m and 0.66 at $\pm$0.5~m, while the radar test alone gives 0.20. The radar test acts only on the dry side. It rejects 108 runs, all with peak areas of 89--159~\kmsq, including 32 of the 55 runs that pass the HFL at $\pm$0.5~m, and it never rejects a wet run, so the upper end of the accepted range is set by the HFL alone. This one-sidedness follows from the rule: across the 600 runs the open-land hit rate correlates at +0.90 with peak flooded land, while the open-land and all-land CSI correlate at $-0.93$ and $-0.97$. The hit-rate test therefore favours runs that overpredict. The 22~\kmsq{} lower bound never binds: the driest run still floods 77~\kmsq{} of land at the pass, and bounds of 18, 22 and 26~\kmsq{} accept the same runs.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the analysis choices} $C$ depends on how strict the rule is (Fig.~\ref{fig:uncertainty}b, Table~\ref{tab:robust}). At $\pm$1.0~m it is 0.49 for hit rates of 0.5--0.6 (when the radar test removes at most two runs), 0.58 at 0.7 and 0.63--0.72 at 0.8--0.85; at $\pm$0.5~m it is 0.67--0.83 over the same hit rates. No run reaches a hit rate of 0.9. Replacing the yes/no rule by likelihood weights with $s$ = 0.5~m, the value of $\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$, narrows the 5--95\% interval by 62\% (121 effective runs), in line with the $\pm$1.0~m rule; $s$ = 0.25 and 1.0~m give 79\% and 35\%. The hit-rate power matters little at small $s$ and more at large $s$: with $s$ = 0.5~m, $p$ = 0, 1, 4 and 8 give 66\%, 65\%, 62\% and 55\%. At $\pm$1.0~m, removing the lowest or highest fifth of any factor's range changes $C$ to 0.57--0.64, except that dropping the highest canopy factors lowers the upper end of the accepted range to 190~\kmsq{} and raises $C$ to 0.81. The first 200 runs alone gave 7 runs accepted at $\pm$0.5~m and 89\% narrowing; the full 600 runs widened the accepted range at both ends, a reminder that a small accepted set understates the range.

\subsection{Agreement with the radar map}
\label{sec:radar}
Passing the radar test does not mean the model reproduces the flood pattern. Table~\ref{tab:confusion} gives the full contingency table for the nominal run, for the median accepted run of the 600-run ensemble (rerun with full map output; peak flooded land 166~\kmsq, HFL error +0.31~m) and for the bare-earth proxy, with the score ranges of all 73 runs accepted at $\pm$1.0~m. On all land the median accepted run catches 44\% of the radar-flooded cells, but more than nine out of ten of its own flooded cells are dry on the radar (false-alarm ratio 0.93), giving a CSI of 0.069 and a frequency bias of 5.8. On open land, where C-band radar should see standing water, the hit rate is 0.78 but the bias is still 8.1. The 73 runs accepted at $\pm$1.0~m have an all-land CSI of 0.059--0.076 and an open-land CSI of 0.080--0.098, and no run of the 600 exceeds 0.081 on all land. A CSI below 0.1 with a false-alarm ratio above 0.9 means poor spatial agreement: the accepted runs reproduce most of the open-land flooding that the radar sees, but most of what they flood is dry on the radar. On open land alone the median accepted run has 695 false-alarm cells (28~\kmsq) against 74 hits, and the open-land false-alarm ratio of the 73 accepted runs is 0.90--0.92. Model-only flooding covers the whole lower floodplain, not only the tree-covered land (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}). Against radar maps made with other thresholds (18--26~\kmsq{} of open-water flooding) and with a fraction cut-off of 0.3, the all-land CSI of the 7 accepted runs of the first 200, for which full maps were kept, stays between 0.048 and 0.12. These tests show that the low CSI is not removed by any classification choice we tried; they do not show that the radar map is accurate. We read the low CSI as a limitation of the model, examined in Section~\ref{sec:drainage}.

\subsection{Why the land stays flooded after the peak}
\label{sec:drainage}
The structural variants of Section~\ref{sec:structural} and the first part of this diagnosis use nominal parameters; the median accepted run is analysed separately below, and the two are not combined.
Mass is conserved (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), so the excess flooding is water that the model keeps on the land, not water it creates. In the nominal run 179.8~\kmsq{} of land is flooded at the radar pass and 355~\Mcm{} is stored on it. Of the flooded land, 128.1~\kmsq{} lies in closed depressions below their spill level, holding 250~\Mcm{} at a mean depth of 1.95~m (Table~\ref{tab:drainage}, Fig.~\ref{fig:drainage}a); 100.9~\kmsq{} of it was already ponded 24~h earlier. A further 38.0~\kmsq{} is still draining and 13.6~\kmsq{} is connected but almost static. Closed-depression cells account for 120.5 of the 168.6~\kmsq{} of false alarms, and 77\% of them are under tree cover. Of the 128.1~\kmsq, 34.5~\kmsq{} lies next to channel cells, where the averaged face bed level can sit below the floodplain raster; the other 93.6~\kmsq{} are depressions in the floodplain terrain itself, with a median depth of 2.9~m (10--90\%: 0.95--7.3~m).

The closed-depression class is insensitive to its threshold: with depression depths of 0.05--0.5~m it covers 124.6--128.1~\kmsq, or 90.1--93.6~\kmsq{} without the cells next to channels. The level-fall threshold only moves land between the draining and slow classes (draining 21.6--40.4~\kmsq{} for thresholds of 0.2--0.05~m). The cells next to channels hold 96 of the 250~\Mcm{} in closed depressions, so they matter more for storage than for area. The median accepted run behaves the same way: of its 128.0~\kmsq{} flooded at the pass, 97.6~\kmsq{} (34.5 of them next to channel cells) is in closed depressions, holding 202 of 271~\Mcm. The depressions are a property of the DSM-derived terrain, not of one parameter set. For every canopy factor from 0.3 to 1.0 the model terrain below 20~m contains 352--379~\kmsq{} of closed depressions deeper than 0.1~m, with 1,067--1,255~\Mcm{} of storage; the bare-earth proxy still has 298~\kmsq{} and 981~\Mcm{} (Table~\ref{tab:drainage}). This is why neither the canopy factor nor the bare-earth proxy removes the excess flooding.

Filling every closed floodplain depression to its spill level and rerunning the nominal model removes nearly half of the water stored on land at the pass (355 to 187~\Mcm) but only 15\% of the flooded land (179.8 to 153.0~\kmsq). The water that no longer ponds spreads as shallow sheets over flat land: the connected-but-slow class grows from 13.6 to 57.6~\kmsq{} at a mean depth of 0.59~m. The remaining closed-depression cells (48.2~\kmsq) lie mostly next to channel cells (31.9~\kmsq). CSI barely changes (0.058 to 0.061 on all land; 0.078 to 0.085 on open land), and the HFL error rises from +2.44 to +2.66~m consistent with less floodplain storage near the gauge. Closed depressions therefore explain most of the water held on the land but not most of the excess extent. We find no single blocked path: the excess is spread over the lower floodplain, in DSM depressions and on low-gradient land that the model drains slowly without the canals, sluices, infiltration and evaporation of the real floodplain. These are mechanisms diagnosed in the model, not verified real-world causes: the classes come from the same terrain and simulation that they describe, and some diagnosed depressions may be artefacts of the surface model rather than real isolated water bodies. We class the excess flooding as an unresolved structural discrepancy of the open-data model.

\subsection{Supplementary ensembles}
\label{sec:fixed}
All 92 fixed-factor runs, all 200 eight-factor runs, all 23 runs with the multiplicative release form, all 46 runs with other travel times, all 140 Morris runs and the 6 repeat runs completed; none failed. Table~\ref{tab:supp} compares them with the main ensemble.

\paragraph{Release formulation} With the multiplicative form, 22 of the 23 runs change peak flooded land by less than 1~\kmsq{} and 13 change the HFL error by less than 0.01~m. Three runs change more: their HFL errors move by $-0.32$, $+0.29$ and $+0.76$~m, and one of them gains 37~\kmsq{} of peak flooded land. The model is deterministic (a repeated run reproduces its original to the last digit), so these are real responses to a forcing change of less than 1\%, not noise; they show that the peak level at the gauge responds unevenly to small changes in the inflow. 21 of the 23 runs remain accepted at $\pm$0.5~m (152--198~\kmsq) and 22 at $\pm$1.0~m (152--219~\kmsq).

\paragraph{Release travel times} With the shorter lags (4 and 2~h), 21 of the 23 runs change peak flooded land by less than 1~\kmsq{} and 17 change the HFL error by less than 0.01~m; the largest changes are +0.29~m and +37~\kmsq, in the same run that jumped under the multiplicative form. All 23 runs stay accepted at $\pm$1.0~m (146--219~\kmsq) and 21 at $\pm$0.5~m (146--198~\kmsq). With the longer lags (12 and 6~h), 22 runs change peak land by less than 1~\kmsq{} and 12 change the HFL error by less than 0.01~m; three runs move by $-0.33$, +0.19 and +0.64~m, and the last of these gains 62~\kmsq{} (160 to 221~\kmsq) and its flooded land now peaks during the main flood wave (hour 105) instead of 36~h later. That run (HFL error +1.05~m) is the only one rejected, so 22 runs remain accepted at both $\pm$0.5 and $\pm$1.0~m (146--198~\kmsq). The assumed travel times therefore change the accepted interval by less than the choice between the two tolerances, but they show the same uneven gauge response as the release formulation: a forcing change of about 1\% can move single runs by up to 0.6~m.

\paragraph{Fixed factors around the accepted runs} When channel roughness, the Idamalayar release and sea level are varied around each of the 23 strictly accepted runs, 57 of the 92 runs pass at $\pm$1.0~m and 30 at $\pm$0.5~m, and 22 of the 23 parent runs keep at least one accepted variant. The accepted peak flooded land spans 146--214~\kmsq{} at $\pm$1.0~m, against 146--198~\kmsq{} for the parents and 146--243~\kmsq{} for the main ensemble. Of the 35 rejected variants, 25 fail the radar test and 10 have HFL errors above +1.0~m. Channel roughness decides most of this: the rejected variants have a mean multiplier of 0.87 and the accepted ones 1.08, because smoother channels drain the floodplain faster. The change in HFL error from the parent run ranges from $-0.70$ to $+2.34$~m and a linear fit on the three factors explains only 20\% of it, again showing an uneven response of the gauge level. Depth and floodplain roughness still compensate each other among the accepted variants (correlation 0.67).

\paragraph{All eight factors varied} In the 200-run eight-factor ensemble, 47 runs pass the HFL at $\pm$1.0~m, 162 pass the radar test and 22 pass both. The accepted runs span 150--210~\kmsq, against a like-for-like prior interval of 111--335~\kmsq, so $C$ = 0.73 (bootstrap 0.71--0.79, 15--29 accepted runs), compared with 0.62 for the five-factor ensemble. The HFL alone gives 0.62 and the radar alone 0.17, so the HFL still does most of the work. At $\pm$0.5~m only 3 runs are accepted, too few for a stable estimate; at $\pm$1.5~m, 47 runs give $C$ = 0.54 (0.53 in the main ensemble). The accepted runs cover nearly the full range of the three added factors (channel roughness 0.76--1.30, Idamalayar release 0.80--1.19, sea level $-0.14$ to $+0.46$~m). With channel roughness free, the correlation between channel depth and floodplain roughness among the accepted runs falls from 0.60 to 0.16: channel roughness takes over part of the compensation. Fixing the three factors therefore did not inflate the contraction; the five-factor result is the more conservative of the two.

\paragraph{HFL observation operator} Across the 484 supplementary runs that recorded them, the nearest cell, the medians within 300, 750 and 1,000~m and the highest cell within 500~m differ from the main operator by at most 0.31~m in runs within $\pm$1.5~m of the HFL, apart from two of the travel-time runs (0.41 and 0.44~m); larger differences, up to 0.90~m, occur only in Morris runs that are 1.2--3.8~m below the HFL. The lowest single-cell peak is on average 0.23~m lower (by up to 1.58~m). In the eight-factor ensemble the nearest cell, the medians and the highest cell accept 20--22 runs over the same 150--210~\kmsq; only the lowest-cell operator accepts more (28 runs, up to 252~\kmsq). The three runs whose HFL error jumped under the multiplicative form were repeated in both forms and reproduced their earlier results exactly. In one of them the jump (+0.29~m) is the same under every operator (+0.28 to +0.30~m), so the whole reach near the gauge rises. In the other two the jumps ($-0.32$ and +0.76~m) appear under the nearest-cell, median and highest-cell operators ($-0.18$ to $-0.57$~m and +0.65 to +0.89~m) but hardly under the lowest cell (+0.01 and +0.12~m), so most channel cells around the gauge rise or fall together while the lowest one barely changes. In all three runs the jump is therefore a response of the river level near the gauge, not an artefact of the operator used to read it. The choice of operator therefore matters much less than the tolerance.

\paragraph{Screening without the release factors} A second Morris design (20 trajectories, 140 runs) holds both release multipliers at 1 and varies the other six factors over the same ranges. Peak flooded land spans 86--399~\kmsq. Channel depth ($\mu^*$ = 164~\kmsq) and floodplain roughness (103~\kmsq) remain first and second for peak land, peak volume, the gauge level and flooded land at the radar pass, and they lead the gauge level in the opposite order (2.99 and 2.56~m). The canopy factor (76~\kmsq) moves ahead of rating-curve error (56~\kmsq) for peak land, but their bootstrap confidence intervals ($\pm$33 and $\pm$19~\kmsq) overlap, so the two are not separable; channel roughness stays fifth (38~\kmsq) and sea level last (6~\kmsq). Removing the release factors therefore does not change which factors dominate, and the release factors do not hide or mimic the effect of channel geometry or roughness. The $\mu^*$ values are not comparable one to one with Table~\ref{tab:morris}, because the trajectories differ.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the observations can and cannot do}
The two observations did two different jobs. They exposed structural errors that the GSA could not, and the HFL then contracted the range of predicted peak flood area within the ensemble by 62\% at the central $\pm$1.0~m tolerance and 77\% at the strict $\pm$0.5~m. The radar scene added little to this (alone, $C$ = 0.20), and only by removing dry runs. Neither observation made the model spatially right: the accepted runs reproduce most of the open-land flooding that the radar sees, but flood five to six times too much land at the radar pass. The contraction is therefore a statement about parameter uncertainty given this model structure, these fixed factors and these factor ranges, and not about predictive accuracy. The v1 screening ranked the right factors on a model that was 10~m wrong, which is a warning for GSA studies without validation.

\paragraph{How robust the contraction is to fixed choices}
The supplementary ensembles (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}) test the choices that the main ensemble held fixed. Varying all eight factors gives $C$ = 0.73 rather than 0.62, because channel roughness takes over part of the depth--roughness compensation and the accepted runs span a narrower range; fixing three factors therefore made the main result conservative, not optimistic. The ranking of the leading factors does not depend on the release factors. The release formulation, the release travel times and the choice among reasonable gauge operators change the accepted range by less than the step from $\pm$0.5 to $\pm$1.0~m. The tolerance is therefore the analysis choice that matters most, and it rests on an observation-error scenario rather than a documented datum. One finding is less reassuring: in a deterministic model, forcing changes of about 1\% moved single runs by up to 0.76~m at the gauge, and by similar amounts under all but the lowest-cell operator. The accepted set near the tolerance edge is therefore sensitive to small forcing changes, and $C$ is best read to the first decimal place.

\paragraph{Structural discrepancy}
The ensemble excludes several sources of error: terrain error beyond the canopy factor (buildings, embankments, DSM noise), channel geometry beyond a uniform depth below the cell minimum, grid resolution, local rainfall and lateral inflows, infiltration and evaporation, and canals and sluices. The diagnosis in Section~\ref{sec:drainage} shows that the excess post-peak flooding is held on the land by closed depressions in the DSM-derived terrain and by slow drainage of low-gradient land, and that no single terrain change we made removes it. The gauge level also shifts by 0.9~m between grids, about $2\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$. A structural error term of that size would lower the contraction further, towards the 35\% of the $s$ = 1.0~m weighting. We therefore read 62\% as a conditional result, and the 35--79\% spread across likelihood weightings as a measure of how much it depends on analysis choices, not as a confidence interval.

\paragraph{Channel geometry leads the ranking}
Channel depth leads peak area, volume and the gauge level on both model versions, so the lack of bathymetry is the largest parameter uncertainty. Measured depths would narrow the range of accepted parameters, and would loosen the depth--roughness compensation, but they would not by themselves remove the excess flooding, which comes from the floodplain terrain.

\paragraph{Canopy correction}
The canopy factor ranks fourth for peak area, and full canopy removal lowers the HFL error by 1.1~m. The accepted runs favour removing most of the canopy height (median factor 0.78) but do not exclude 0.32. Because the range of this factor is exploratory, we read this as a preference rather than an estimate. DSM-based models of vegetated tropical floodplains should treat the correction as an explicit uncertain factor. A bare-earth product such as FABDEM \citep{hawker2022} is worth testing, but our proxy still has 298~\kmsq{} of closed depressions, so a bare-earth DEM alone should not be expected to fix the drainage.

\paragraph{Equifinality}
Channel depth and floodplain roughness compensate each other (correlation 0.60 in the 73 accepted runs), so one HFL mark cannot separate them, in line with the equifinality that motivates GLUE \citep{beven1992} and with the trade-off between channel depth and friction found when calibrating a hydraulic model against radar flood extents \citep{wood2016}. A second gauge or an image taken near the peak could separate them.

\paragraph{Radar limits}
Of the land cells, 75.8\% are tree cover and 14.9\% built-up, where C-band radar sees little open water, and the only scene was taken 5 days after the peak. Uncertainty in the satellite flood map itself also limits how strongly it can test a model \citep{stephens2012}. These limits explain why the radar can serve only as a lower bound with a hit-rate test \citep[rather than as a binary pattern to match;][]{aronica2002}, and why the test favours wetter runs. They do not explain the open-land false alarms, which come from the drainage behaviour of the model (Section~\ref{sec:drainage}).

\paragraph{What transfers}
What transfers to other basins is the workflow, not the numbers: an open-data model built and run with HYDROLIB-core, validation used to revise the structure before screening, a Morris screening repeated on the final structure, an ensemble conditioned on whatever sparse observations exist, and a report of the conditional contraction with every interval, every observation's contribution and the structural tests that bound it. The 62--77\% contraction, the factor ranking and the accepted ranges are results for this basin, this model and these two observations. For other forested monsoon floodplains we state three hypotheses rather than findings: that a single high-flood mark constrains the predicted area mainly through channel depth and floodplain roughness and leaves them in compensation; that a post-peak radar scene over tree cover rejects the driest runs but not the wettest; and that a DSM-based floodplain will hold water in closed depressions after the peak. The omitted processes could shift this balance. Infiltration and evaporation would let water leave the depressions and lower the flooded area at the pass, which could make a radar image more informative. Explicit dam operation would change the timing of the release factors. Rainfall on the floodplain would add water locally.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Excess post-peak flooding. At the radar pass every run still floods 77--250~\kmsq{} of land against 22~\kmsq{} of open water on the radar. It is held in DSM depressions and on low-gradient land (Section~\ref{sec:drainage}) and remains an unresolved structural discrepancy.
\item The tide is synthetic, and there is no local rainfall, lateral inflow, infiltration or evaporation on the model domain. The tide tests and the Morris screening show a negligible effect of sea level, for these tests and this model only.
\item Boundary flows were digitised from report figures, and the gauge location was inferred from the water mask. The HFL datum is not documented in the three CWC reports we searched; we assume it is close to mean sea level, so $\sigma_\mathrm{obs}$ and the tolerances are a scenario, not a measured error. The release travel times (8 and 4~h) are assumed; tests with half and 1.5 times these values change the accepted range by less than the tolerance step.
\item The radar classification has no urban, shadow, layover or vegetation mask, and its accuracy is unknown; the 22~\kmsq{} is a conservative count.
\item The model uses 200~m cells and one event; the bare-earth test uses a proxy, not FABDEM.
\item Factor ranges are expert assumptions, sampled independently; the channel depth and canopy ranges are exploratory. In the main ensemble three factors were held at nominal values; they make up 11\% of the summed Morris $\mu^*$ for area (a share of $\mu^*$, not of variance). Varying them gives a larger contraction (0.73), but the eight-factor ensemble has only 200 runs and 22 accepted runs. All results are conditional on these choices.
\item The gauge level responds unevenly to small forcing changes in some runs (up to 0.76~m for a change of about 1\%), so acceptance near the tolerance edge is not smooth in the factors.
\item The model has not been validated for operational use. It was tested against one gauge mark and one radar scene for one event.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We built an open-data D-Flow FM model of the 2018 Periyar flood with HYDROLIB-core, screened it with 180 Morris runs and conditioned a 600-run Latin hypercube on one high-flood level and one Sentinel-1 scene. Validation against the same two observations exposed three structural errors and one missing tributary that the sensitivity analysis did not. On the final model, channel depth, floodplain roughness and rating-curve error lead the flooded area, as they did before the last revision, and sea level is negligible. Conditioning contracted the range of peak flooded land within the ensemble by 62\% with an HFL tolerance of $\pm$1.0~m, about twice the estimated observation error, and by 77\% with $\pm$0.5~m, to 146--243 and 146--198~\kmsq. The high-flood level does most of this; the radar test removes only dry runs, and channel depth and floodplain roughness stay in compensation. Varying all eight factors gives a larger contraction (73\%), and the release formulation, release travel times and gauge operator matter less than the tolerance, which rests on an assumed datum. These figures are conditional on the model structure, the fixed factors, the factor ranges and the acceptance rule, and they are not a reduction of predictive error. The accepted runs still flood far more land than the radar shows after the peak, because the DSM-based terrain holds water in closed depressions and drains low land slowly; filling the depressions does not remove the excess, and we leave it as an unresolved structural discrepancy. The model has not been validated for operational use. What we offer for other basins is the workflow, with its explicit reporting of intervals, contributions and structural limits, rather than the numbers.
